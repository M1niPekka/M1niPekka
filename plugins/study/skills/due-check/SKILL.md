# Due check

The value here is completeness and honesty, not formatting. A student asking
"what am I forgetting" is asking you to find the thing they've lost track of.

## Gather from every available source

- **Calendar** — assignment due dates, test dates, project deadlines.
- **Email** — teacher announcements, assignment postings, schedule changes.
  Recent unread mail from school domains is high-signal.
- **Drive / files** — documents with a due date in them, half-finished drafts.
  A doc last edited two weeks ago with a deadline this week is exactly the
  thing they've forgotten.
- **Anything they've told you** earlier in the conversation or in past sessions.

If a source isn't connected, say which one and what you might be missing, so
they know the list has a known hole rather than assuming it's complete.

## Report

Order by **urgency and risk**, not by date alone. A big project due Friday that
hasn't been started outranks a worksheet due tomorrow that takes ten minutes.

For each item give: what it is, when it's due, and your read on where it stands
(not started / in progress / nearly done) with the evidence for that read.

Then call out, separately and explicitly:

- **At risk** — big, close, and no sign of progress. Be direct about this; a
  soft phrasing here defeats the point of the whole check.
- **Quick wins** — things that would take under 20 minutes and can be cleared
  today.
- **Conflicts** — two deadlines on one day, a test the morning after a big
  project is due. Naming a collision a few days early is the most useful thing
  in the report.

## Then help

Don't stop at the list. Offer the obvious next step: a `study-plan` for the
test, a working session on the at-risk project, or putting missing deadlines on
the calendar.

Never invent a deadline or a status. If you don't know whether something's been
started, say you don't know and ask.
