# Exam guide

Build a guide the student can actually use in the time they actually have. The
single biggest failure mode is producing a comprehensive content dump when the
test is in 10 hours — comprehensive is the enemy of useful under time pressure.

## Step 1: Establish the three facts

Before writing anything, know:

1. **What test, and how much time is left.** Hours, days, or weeks. This drives
   everything below.
2. **What's actually on it.** Ask for the syllabus, study guide, review sheet,
   or unit name. Check Drive/email/Calendar if connected. Do not guess at scope
   when the student can just tell you — but do not block on it either: for
   standardized tests (SAT, PSAT, AP, ACT) the scope is public and fixed, so
   write the guide and confirm details after.
3. **Where they're weak.** If unknown, cover both/all sections evenly rather
   than stalling.

## Step 2: Pick the mode by time remaining

**Under 24 hours — triage mode.** Cramming new content is low-yield and raises
anxiety. Weight the guide roughly:
- 50% test mechanics and strategy (timing per question, guessing rules, how the
  format works, common traps)
- 30% high-yield facts worth memorizing cold (formulas, dates, rules — things
  that fit on one page and pay off immediately)
- 20% logistics and the night-before/morning-of plan (what to bring, sleep,
  food, when to stop studying)
Say plainly what NOT to study. Telling someone to skip a topic is the most
valuable thing you can do the night before.

**2–7 days — targeted mode.** Diagnose first: have them do a few practice
problems per topic, then build the guide around what actually broke. Weight
toward weak areas and active recall, not rereading.

**1+ weeks — build mode.** Full content coverage, spaced across days. Hand off
to the `study-plan` skill for the schedule and keep this guide as the content
reference.

## Step 3: Write it

Rules that hold in every mode:

- **Lead with the mechanics.** Format, timing, scoring, and question count go
  first. A student who knows the shape of the test makes better decisions than
  one who knows more content.
- **Be concrete about timing.** "Roughly 1 minute 15 seconds per question, so
  if you're 3 minutes into one, you're already losing points elsewhere" beats
  "manage your time."
- **Every formula gets a plain-language trigger.** Not just the formula, but
  the sentence in a problem that tells you to reach for it.
- **Name the traps.** Common wrong-answer patterns are worth more than extra
  content, because they convert near-misses into points.
- **No filler.** Cut motivational padding, cut "remember to try your best."
  The student is stressed and scanning.

## Step 4: Deliver it where they can use it

A guide read on a phone at 7am in a car is the actual use case. Publish it as
an artifact so it survives the conversation and works on mobile — scannable
headings, short blocks, no wall of prose. Ask before publishing only if the
content is sensitive.

Offer, at the end, to quiz them on it (`flashcards` skill) rather than assuming
they want more reading.

## What not to do

- Do not write practice questions that imitate a real secured exam's actual
  items. Teach the pattern and write original practice instead.
- Do not help with a test the student is currently taking, or with material
  they've said is off-limits. Preparing beforehand is studying; feeding answers
  during a live exam is cheating — say so plainly and offer to prep instead.
- Do not pad a night-before guide to look thorough. Length is not value here.
